CrowdStrike Signal 上线 Automated Leads 智能排序层

Reprioritizing Automated Leads with CrowdStrike Signal

AI 导读

CrowdStrike Signal 于 2026 年 9 月 9 日为 Automated Leads 上线优先级排序层，通过关联主机异常指标与真实入侵特征，将线索的置信度与攻击相似度纳入排序。

正文 · 原文

In May, we pulled back the curtain on the self-learning AI models behind Automated Leads, an approach to threat detection designed to surface the subtle signs of an attack. It’s powered by CrowdStrike® Signal and delivered from the CrowdStrike Falcon® platform. 

Signal (distinct from SGNL) scores every indicator by how unusual it is for that specific host. Most of these indicators are faint on their own; for example, a PowerShell download or routine administrative command. They fire too often to warrant analyst concern every time. CrowdStrike Signal looks for what genuinely stands out on a given host, whether that’s a single highly unusual indicator or a handful of weaker ones that cluster in time. It correlates them, together with any related detections, into a single lead. A lead can come together from one short burst of activity or build quietly over several days.

In September, we introduced the next step: a new prioritization layer that sits on top of CrowdStrike Signal and changes how those leads are scored and ordered. Generally available since September 9, 2026, it significantly reduces false positives while detecting more malicious activity. 

The Challenge: Most Unusual Activity Is Benign

Unusual activity is everywhere, and most of it is benign: a software rollout, an administrator trying a new tool, a one-off maintenance script. Some of it is detected correctly without being an attack, such as adware or sanctioned red-team exercises. Real intrusions are rare by comparison, and they range from commodity malware to adversaries who make efforts to look like a system administrator.

For a security team with finite time, this creates a ranking problem. When unusual activity never stops, the order in which leads appear determines what gets investigated and what waits.

What’s New in CrowdStrike Signal

CrowdStrike Signal still decides what becomes a lead. The new prioritization layer decides where that lead belongs in the queue.

Previously, a lead's confidence reflected how unusual its activity was, taken as a whole. It now also reflects how closely that activity resembles what real intrusions look like. A noisy software deployment and a quiet intrusion can be equally unusual; the prioritization layer tells them apart, so the intrusion climbs the queue while the deployment drops down or out of it entirely.

What this means for your queue:

  • Reductions of up to 70% in lead volume, with fewer leads to review at every sensitivity setting. Some leads fall off the queue altogether; some are still available at lower thresholds, which you can choose to view with the confidence threshold control.
  • Better coverage of real threats: Activity that CrowdStrike threat hunters confirm as malicious still reaches the queue, and more of it does at the Moderate and Cautious settings.

How It Works: AI Agents on the Endpoint

AI agents are an emerging attack surface, and adversaries are already moving on it: The CrowdStrike 2026 Threat Hunting Report documents STARDUST CHOLLIMA poisoning AI framework packages. Agents running on tools such as Claude Code and ChatGPT Codex now run shell commands on endpoints across the business, frequently with broad permissions and without a person approving each step. While most of their actions are routine, an agent that is misused, prompt-injected, or hijacked runs the same ordinary commands an attacker would, with the full access of the user behind it. This activity can be difficult to identify as malicious. 

With Automated Leads, CrowdStrike Signal can identify malicious activity without knowing whether an agent was involved. It watches what happens on the host and raises a lead when activity stands out, whether the commands came from a person, a piece of malware, or an AI agent. Recently detected Automated Leads, as detailed below, show what this looks like in practice. The intent behind the activity varied from case to case, but the path to the analyst was the same each time: The agent's actions reached the host as ordinary commands, CrowdStrike Signal correlated the activity that was out of character for that host into a lead, and the prioritization layer moved the lead to the top of the queue because the pattern matched what past intrusions also showed.

This capability complements CrowdStrike® Falcon Guardian™, which secures agents directly by discovering them, controlling which ones may run, and tracing each prompt to what happened on the system. Security teams using both can view malicious activity within agents or on the endpoint.

Are AI Agents Working for You or Against You?

Across these leads, AI agents did many of the things an intruder does in the first hours of an intrusion. They tested administrator passwords against other hosts. They swept workstations for stored credentials, mapped shares, SSH keys without passphrases, and domain trusts. They audited which accounts hold the directory replication rights that DCSync attacks abuse. One self-hosted agent installed its own persistence, slipped out of the sandbox meant to contain it, and pushed commands into a cloud virtual desktop through the clipboard. In intrusions that threat hunters attributed to likely LLM-driven attackers, the same kind of reconnaissance ran under compromised servers: On one Windows web and database server, the SQL Server service began issuing identity, privilege, and network discovery commands that it had not run before.

Some of this work was sanctioned administration or authorized security testing. Some looked like an internal audit whose authorization wasn’t recorded, and some were real intrusions. In other cases, the actions alone did not clearly reveal whether a benign user had asked for them or a malicious prompt had steered the agent.

Most of these actions would not justify an alert on their own. The indicators behind them, such as domain-trust enumeration with nltest.exe, an antivirus inventory query in PowerShell, or remote authentication from a Python script, fire constantly across any large environment as administrators, build systems, and coding agents go about their work. Alerting on every occurrence would bury a security team in noise. What set these cases apart was where and how the indicators appeared: on hosts where they were out of character (for example, a database service that does not normally launch commands), and in sequences that resemble the opening moves of an intrusion.

Under the previous scoring, several of these leads sat with low confidence in the triage queues, and some were visible only at the most sensitive settings. With the prioritization layer, each moved into the lowest-volume Cautious band and to the top of its queue, often while the activity was still at the reconnaissance stage, where an analyst could quickly decide whether the agent was doing its job or someone else's.

How to Use It

The prioritization layer is enabled for all customers as of September 9, 2026. No configuration is required.

  1. In the Automated Leads dashboard, sort by Confidence to review the leads that most closely resemble real attacks first.
  2. Adjust the sensitivity in Automated lead settings (Extra Aggressive, Aggressive, Moderate, Cautious) to choose how far down the queue to work.

Because every setting now surfaces fewer leads, teams that chose a conservative setting to keep volume manageable may want to revisit that choice. A more sensitive setting now brings in less work than it did before, while catching more of what matters.

What's Next

We are expanding the range of attacks the prioritization layer recognizes, keeping confidence current as a lead evolves, and feeding analyst verdicts back into prioritization so it keeps pace with how adversaries operate. The goal has not changed: The leads at the top of an analyst's queue should be the ones that matter.
