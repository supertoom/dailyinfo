AWS 解析 DKIM 重放攻击原理并给出 Amazon SES 邮件处置方案

Beyond DKIM key rotation: understanding email replay attacks

AI 导读

Amazon SES 可通过消息事件、DMARC 聚合报告和 DNS 查询日志发现异常，但仅凭聚合数据不能确认重放；需将已发送邮件与未联系收件人收到的邮件进行消息级匹配，并排除合法中继。

正文 · 原文

As the email administrator for your organization’s domain, you receive a Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (DMARC) aggregate report showing email passing DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM) verification as though it came from your domain, sent from IP addresses you don’t recognize to recipients you didn’t contact. To clear the issue, you rotate your DKIM key, confirm the new selector is live, and verify that your DMARC policy is already set to p=reject. Days later, you see that the same unfamiliar traffic is still authenticating as you.

In this post, you learn why rotation alone can’t stop a DKIM replay attack, how to confirm that you’re looking at a replay rather than a legitimate relay or ordinary spoofing, and the DNS cleanup step that most rotation guidance leaves out. You also learn how you can use Amazon Simple Email Service (Amazon SES) to detect and respond to this activity faster.

How DKIM replay works

DKIM verifies one thing: that specific parts of a message didn’t change between the signer and the receiver. The signer signs the message with a private key, and receivers fetch the matching public key from your domain’s DNS to check the signature. A valid signature continues to validate wherever the message travels afterward. Mailing lists, .forward rules, and third-party relays all preserve it, which is the protocol working as designed.

A replay attack takes advantage of that durability. The attack works as follows:

  1. Your ecommerce platform sends a customer a legitimate response email, signed with your DKIM key.
  2. An attacker obtains one copy of that message. They might subscribe to your mailing list, compromise a mailbox, retrieve it from a list archive, or capture it through a forwarding rule.
  3. The attacker resends that message, unchanged, from their own servers to thousands of new recipients.
  4. Each receiving server fetches your public key from DNS and checks the signature. The signature passes, because the body wasn’t modified. Sender Policy Framework (SPF) fails, because the sending IP isn’t yours, but DMARC requires only one of SPF or DKIM to pass with alignment. Because DKIM passes, DMARC passes, and the message is delivered as authenticated email from your domain.

Understanding what an attacker can and can’t do matters, because the limits define the problem. An attacker can’t modify your message, add new content, or read your email. The signature is only valid while the message remains byte-for-byte identical.

What an attacker gains is your domain’s reputation, which they use to deliver your genuine message to an audience you didn’t intend. A receipt or invoice becomes a lure, and email that would normally be filtered is delivered because receivers trust your domain. This is abuse of reputation and reach, not content forgery, and the distinction matters when you explain the incident to your security team.

This also explains why an enforcing DMARC policy doesn’t stop an attack. DMARC passes when SPF or DKIM passes with alignment. During a replay, DKIM passes, so DMARC passes, and p=reject does not apply. A p=reject policy is designed to stop spoofing, where a forger has no valid signature and DKIM fails. It does nothing against a message that you signed. Determining which of the two was used is what tells you how to respond.

Is it replay, spoofing, or a legitimate relay?

Email from an unfamiliar IP address that passes DKIM has three common explanations, and each requires a different response. Confirm which you’re dealing with before you make any changes.

DMARC aggregate (rua) reports provide the source IP address, message volume, and SPF and DKIM results. This is enough to identify an anomaly, but not to prove a replay. A report row might read as follows:

source IP: 203.0.113.45 | count: 4,200 | SPF: fail | DKIM: pass (d=yourdomain.com)

You need to evaluate that row against the three possibilities:

  • The first possibility is a legitimate relay that you haven’t inventoried, such as a mailing list, a forwarder, or an email security gateway such as an inline cloud access security broker (CASB) that you route through. The signature validly survives the relay, and this is normal email flow. In the report, the source IP address usually resolves to a known provider, and the volume tracks your actual sending.
  • The second possibility is replay abuse, in which an attacker redistributes a message that you legitimately signed. In this case, the DKIM-passing volume comes from an IP address that has no business relationship with you, often well above the usual number of recipients that you send to, and frequently to recipient domains that you haven’t worked with.
  • The third possibility is ordinary spoofing, which is a forgery with no valid signature. That row shows DKIM: fail. DKIM that passes from an unknown IP address is therefore usually not ordinary spoofing.

Aggregate counts can’t prove a replay on their own. To confirm a replay, you need message-level evidence: a specific message that you sent matched to one that reached recipients you didn’t address. If you can’t make that match, treat the activity as an unconfirmed anomaly, and begin with the enforcement and monitoring steps that follow rather than removing keys.

Set up an enforcing DMARC policy first

Before you take any other action, confirm that you’re enforcing DMARC. Many domains that encounter this problem are still at p=none, which is monitoring only. This situation is common. Every DMARC deployment begins at p=none, and many organizations do not advance beyond it because nothing appears to be broken. At p=none, receivers take no action on failures, so no change you make affects delivery, and you can’t determine what enforcement would have caught.

The first step is therefore not key removal; it’s completing your sender inventory and reaching enforcement:

  1. Inventory every legitimate sender and relay for your domain, including your email service providers, mailing lists, and any security gateways that send on your behalf.
  2. Bring each sender into SPF and DKIM alignment so that legitimate email authenticates.
  3. Advance the policy one stage at a time, from p=none to p=quarantine to p=reject. Review your rua reports at each stage before you advance, typically for a week or two per stage, and longer if you have many senders to work through. The pct tag lets you ramp up gradually, for example by quarantining 25% of failing email before you increase the percentage.

After you’re enforcing DMARC, receivers that honor DMARC drop unauthenticated spoofing on your behalf. Only after this baseline is in place, and only after you have confirmed a replay of genuinely signed email, does the next step apply.

Remove the old key from DNS: routine hygiene after rotation

Most rotation guidance omits this step. Rotating a key and removing the old key are two separate actions.

Rotation means that your provider begins signing new email with a new key and selector. Removal means deleting the old public key from DNS, so that previously signed messages, including the copies that an attacker captured, can no longer be verified. If you rotate but leave the old public key published, every message ever signed with that key remains replayable for as long as the record exists. Removing the old key is what closes that window and is the step that’s commonly overlooked.

Removal affects only messages that are still in transit and were signed with the old key. After email is delivered, the receiver no longer needs your key, so we recommend that you leave the old key published long enough for in-flight email to arrive and then remove it. How much of this process you manage yourself depends on your DKIM method, which the following section describes. During a confirmed active replay, closing this window is essential.

How you manage keys depends on the DKIM method that you use with Amazon SES. With Bring Your Own DKIM (BYODKIM), you generate the key pair and publish the public key as a TXT record that you control. You can rotate and remove keys whenever you want, including removing an old key on demand during an incident. As the BYODKIM documentation states, “you can rotate the DKIM keys for your domains as often as you want.”

With Easy DKIM, a feature where Amazon SES generates and manages the keys for you, you publish CNAME records that point to AWS-hosted keys. Because your DNS points to AWS-hosted keys through those CNAME records, Amazon SES manages the underlying key material for you, so you don’t publish or remove individual keys yourself. Unlike BYODKIM, Easy DKIM doesn’t provide an on-demand option to delete a specific key immediately.

If you’re an Easy DKIM sender during a confirmed replay, your defenses are early detection (described in the following section), the duplicate-message throttling that receivers already apply, and, over the longer term, the emerging DKIM2 effort, an IETF initiative to update the DKIM specification with built-in replay resistance, which is being designed to give receivers better signals to identify and act on replayed messages at the protocol level.

How Amazon SES helps you detect and respond

Your first response is detection, not DNS changes. The goal is to identify anomalous DKIM-passing volume early, while you build the message-level evidence that distinguishes a replay from a legitimate relay.

Begin by enabling logging. Create an Amazon SES configuration set with an event destination (see Monitor email sending using Amazon SES event publishing) that streams per-message events (Send, Delivery, Bounce, and Complaint) to Amazon Data Firehose or Amazon EventBridge (see Add an event destination), tagged with the From domain.

Separately, confirm that you’re receiving rua reports for your domain. You enable these reports by adding a rua= address to your domain’s DMARC TXT record, for example rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.com. For the record syntax and a phased p=none to p=quarantine to p=reject rollout, see Complying with DMARC authentication protocol in Amazon SES. Reports arrive as daily XML digests, and most teams process them with a DMARC analyzer rather than reading the raw XML.

With both sources in place, you can correlate them. Compare the email that your account emitted—based on your Amazon SES delivery events—against what DMARC reports show that receivers accepted as your domain. When DMARC shows DKIM-passing volume from IP addresses outside your SPF record that exceeds the delivery events that Amazon SES recorded for that From domain in the same window, that gap is your signal. It’s also how you build the message-level case that distinguishes a replay from a legitimate relay.

We recommend three additional controls. Amazon SES sending authorization restricts which AWS principals can send using your verified identity. It doesn’t stop an external replay, but it addresses the insider and third-party vector that SPF, DKIM, and DMARC don’t cover on their own, so use identity-level policies to limit sending to specific AWS Identity and Access Management (IAM) principals.

Use Amazon Route 53 to set short DNS time-to-live values (TTLs) (for example, 300 seconds) on your DKIM records so that changes propagate quickly; and enable DNS query logging with an Amazon CloudWatch Logs metric filter that watches for queries to a selector that you’ve rotated away from.

A spike in lookups for an old selector is a useful early indicator of a replay attempt using a retired selector. For a BYODKIM domain during a confirmed replay, Route 53 is also where you remove the old selector’s TXT record. You can automate that removal by using an Amazon CloudWatch alarm that starts an AWS Lambda function to call the Route 53 API and retire an old selector’s record.

Scope the function’s IAM role to only the specific Route 53 hosted zone and record that it needs to change, so that it follows least privilege. Because an incorrect DNS change can have a wide effect, gate any automation behind confirmed-replay criteria and test it against a non-production domain first. For most teams, alarm-to-human is safer than alarm-to-automatic-removal.

When your domain is the one being abused

Most guidance assumes that you’re either the provider preventing abuse or the receiver detecting it. In a replay, your domain is the one whose reputation is being consumed, and you aren’t on the recipient list. As a result, you don’t have the message that everyone asks for when you report the issue.

When you file an abuse report with the provider that hosts the source IP address, they ask for full message headers. The email went to people you didn’t contact, so you don’t have the message headers. You then provide DMARC aggregate reports that show the source IP address and volume, and the provider asks for headers again. At this point, you’re unable to proceed.

To break this cycle, go to the recipients. Review your rua reports for the top recipient domains—which are often .gov and .edu domains—contact those recipients directly and request a sample message with full headers. In addition to providing the headers, that sample lets you confirm a replay rather than a legitimate relay. Build your abuse report around the IP address and the DMARC evidence rather than the message content. If the source IP address belongs to Amazon Web Services (AWS), report it through the AWS abuse process and include your DMARC reports and SPF record as evidence.

Incident response: ordered actions

If your reports show suspicious DKIM-passing email from unfamiliar IP addresses, complete the following procedure.

To respond to a suspected DKIM replay

  1. Confirm that it’s a replay. Match a message that you sent to one that reached recipients you didn’t address and rule out legitimate relays and gateways. Aggregate data alone isn’t proof.
  2. Reach an enforcing DMARC policy. Inventory your legitimate senders, align them, and advance from p=none to p=quarantine to p=reject. This stops spoofing.
  3. Harden SPF to -all rather than ~all, but only after your sender inventory is complete, since -all hard-fails any legitimate source you haven’t listed.
  4. Check the DKIM-Signature header of a fresh message to confirm that your provider signs only with the current selector.
  5. Report the source IP address to the hosting provider and include your DMARC evidence.
  6. Investigate the capture point, such as a compromised mailbox, a mailing list, a forwarding rule, or a newsletter subscription.
  7. Remove the old DKIM public key after in-flight email has cleared. For BYODKIM, delete the old selector’s TXT record. For Easy DKIM, rely on the managed Amazon SES rotation together with the monitoring and receiver-side defenses described earlier.

The following table summarizes what each action does and doesn’t do.

Action What it does What it doesn’t do
Confirm replay (message-level) Separates a replay from a legitimate relay or spoofing Can’t be done from aggregate DMARC data alone
DMARC enforcement (p=none to p=reject) Drops unauthenticated spoofing Does nothing against a replay because DKIM still passes
Key rotation Signs new email with a new key Doesn’t invalidate already signed messages
Remove old key (routine, post-rotation) Invalidates old-key signatures, including captured ones Doesn’t protect in-flight old-key email; on-demand removal available only with BYODKIM
Amazon SES sending authorization Restricts which principals send as your identity Doesn’t stop external replay of captured messages

Conclusion

DKIM replay succeeds because a valid signature keeps validating, and an enforcing DMARC policy doesn’t change that. To respond effectively, confirm that the activity is a replay with message-level evidence, reach an enforcing DMARC policy, and then remove the old key from DNS as routine post-rotation hygiene. Keep your primary defense on monitoring and DMARC enforcement rather than on emergency DNS changes. The durable fix for replay is receiver-side: duplicate-message counting today, and stronger protocol-level signals through DKIM2 in the future.

To get started, review your current DMARC policy and confirm that you’re receiving rua reports, enable Amazon SES event publishing, and document how you rotate and remove DKIM keys for each of your domains.
