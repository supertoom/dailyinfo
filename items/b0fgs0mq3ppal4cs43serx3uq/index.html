Hackers Using Email Bombing Before Posing as IT Support on Microsoft Teams

AI 导读

攻击者先用订阅邮件轰炸员工工作邮箱，再通过 Microsoft Teams 冒充 IT 支持人员，以协助停止邮件为由诱导员工执行操作，进而试图控制账户或计算机。

正文 · 原文

Fortra warns of an email bombing campaign where hackers flood work inboxes, then pose as IT support on Microsoft Teams to get access to work accounts.

Receiving thousands of unwanted emails can be more than an inbox problem because cybercriminals are using this strategy to approach employees on Microsoft Teams, pretending to be IT support staff and offering help to stop the messages.

Fortra Intelligence and Research Experts (FIRE) have discovered and reported impersonation fraud cases where attackers first bombard employees with subscription emails and then contact them through Teams. The callers claim to be from the company’s IT department and provide instructions to stop the messages. But these instructions are intended to compromise the employee’s account or computer.

The emails are generated through a technique called subscription bombing, in which hackers enter a target’s email address into newsletter sign-up pages, registration forms and other websites; the services then send confirmation emails and other notifications to the address.

According to FIRE’s observations, shared with Hackread.com, these campaigns generate around 1,000 emails a day and up to 10,000 messages are sent during an active campaign. There’s evidence suggesting that attackers may be using AI agents to find websites with sign-up forms and submit email addresses automatically. Mailchimp and its email delivery service Mandrill were the most commonly observed platforms, followed by Amazon SES and SendGrid.

Posing as internal IT staff, these fake callers give victims verbal and email instructions to stop the incoming messages, but Fortra says they are “attempting to compromise the user account, system, or both.”

Gaining access to an employee’s account could expose company emails, files and other resources the account can reach, while access to the computer could also expose other systems. The actual impact would depend on what access the attackers manage to obtain.

Forta researchers also found phishing emails during some of these attacks involving messages that used fake loading screens and verification steps to make their pages look genuine, but they could not conclude whether those behind these emails were the same attackers making the Teams calls.

Explaining the reason behind the approach, FIRE wrote that attackers are “creating the incident and then attempting to own the response.” That’s an important distinction- the employee is not simply dealing with unwanted mail but may also be approached by the person responsible for it.

This is not the first report of Teams being abused by cybercriminals to pose as IT staff. In October, Hackread reported the Arctic Wolf Labs research revealing over 400 incidents involving fake IT support accounts contacting employees through external Teams chats.

Fortra advises employees to avoid attending unexpected support calls and personally contact their IT department through official channels because simply knowing about the email flood doesn’t prove they are genuine IT support staff.

Researchers also suggest that security teams should review external access in Teams, preserve suspicious emails, and immediately check account activity if an employee followed the caller’s instructions.
