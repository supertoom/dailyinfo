认知战：AI、深度伪造与恐惧如何将人类心智变成新战场

Cognitive warfare: the new battlefield is the human mind

AI 导读

正文 · 原文

Cognitive warfare uses AI, deepfakes and fear to manipulate opinions, erode trust and disrupt society.

Contemporary warfare is no longer fought solely through armies, weapons, and technological infrastructure. Alongside military operations and cyberattacks, a new dimension of conflict is emerging in which the primary target is people’s ability to interpret reality, distinguish reliable information from manipulated information, and make autonomous decisions. This is the context in which cognitive warfare takes root, a strategy that aims to influence the perceptions, beliefs, and behaviors of individuals and communities, transforming information into a tool of pressure and destabilization.

Unlike traditional propaganda, cognitive warfare leverages a much more complex technological ecosystem. Social networks, digital platforms, profiling systems, artificial intelligence, and psychological manipulation techniques allow for reaching different audiences with messages crafted to elicit specific reactions. The goal is not necessarily to impose a single narrative, but to influence the process by which people acquire information, form opinions, and evaluate events.

Multiplier of information operations and the manipulation of consensus

Generative artificial intelligence has further expanded the operational possibilities of these campaigns. The automated production of text, images, audio, and video allows for the construction of increasingly credible fake content, simulating statements from public figures, and fueling networks of coordinated accounts. Deepfakes pose a significant problem for information security because they can compromise the reliability of audiovisual evidence and make it more difficult to verify the authenticity of a communication. A significant finding emerges from the fourth annual report of the European External Action Service on FIMI (Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference) threats , published in March 2026. In 2025, 27% of incidents analyzed by the EEAS (European External Action Service) involved techniques attributable to artificial intelligence, with an increase in cases from 41 in 2024 to 147 in 2025, equal to approximately 259% . Artificial intelligence is being used to accelerate content production, translate and adapt messages into multiple languages, generate synthetic images and videos, and amplify influencer efforts through coordinated networks.

In this context, social media platforms represent one of the most favorable environments for the spread of cognitive operations. The speed of content circulation, recommendation systems, and the ability to reach specific communities allow specific narratives to be amplified, even through coordinated behavior. Fake accounts, bots, and seemingly independent profiles can contribute to the impression that a given opinion is widely shared. One of the most insidious strategies isn’t necessarily spreading completely false news, but rather mixing authentic information, manipulated content, and misleading interpretations. This makes it more difficult to distinguish facts from reconstructions constructed to influence the public.

School bomb scares as an example of psychological pressure

A concrete example that provides insight into the psychological dimension of these threats is the bomb threat that affected schools in Italy, Scotland, Northern Ireland, and the Republic of Ireland on November 11, 2026. In Italy, approximately 6,300 schools received communications containing attack threats, prompting evacuations in hundreds of schools and mobilizing law enforcement and emergency personnel. Authorities deemed the reports to be false alarms, with no devices identified. 

The incident deserves attention not only for its international dimension, but above all for the mechanism by which an electronic communication produced concrete consequences. It was not necessary to compromise the schools’ computer networks or damage infrastructure. Sending a single message was enough to activate emergency procedures, interrupt school activities, generate concern among families, and commit public resources. The human factor thus becomes the primary factor through which the threat’s impact materializes. School administrators, teachers, and law enforcement responded in accordance with their responsibilities, while parents and students were exposed to a situation of great uncertainty. Even when the alarm turns out to be unfounded, the psychological and organizational consequences remain real. This episode demonstrates how manipulating the perception of danger can influence collective behavior.

The connection between cognitive warfare and cybersecurity

Cognitive warfare has significant parallels with cybersecurity and hybrid operations. A ransomware attack against strategic infrastructure, a breach of public administration systems, or the theft of confidential documents can have a significance that transcends the technical damage. The stolen information can be selected, manipulated, or released at strategic moments to compromise the reputation of institutions and influence public debate. In this scenario, cybersecurity is not just about protecting networks, devices, and data, but also the ability to manage manipulated information and communications designed to generate fear, disorientation, and mistrust. Countering cognitive warfare requires a multidisciplinary approach. Protecting digital infrastructures must be accompanied by the ability to recognize information manipulation, verify sources, and understand the techniques used to influence people’s behavior. For companies and public administrations, it is becoming important to integrate cyber incident management with timely and transparent communication. 

Cognitive warfare demonstrates that a country’s security does not depend solely on the protection of its information systems or the availability of military capabilities. It also depends on trust in institutions, the quality of information, and the population’s ability to recognize attempts at manipulation. The case of the school bomb threats highlights a fundamental aspect: it’s not always necessary to physically attack an infrastructure to compromise its functioning. In certain circumstances, it’s sufficient to exploit fear and human reactions to generate disorganization and social pressure. The new battlefield, therefore, isn’t just digital. It’s also cognitive, and concerns the ability of individuals and institutions to maintain clarity, trust, and decision-making autonomy in the face of information designed to destabilize them.
