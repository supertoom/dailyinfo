ThreatsDay: 10M NGINX Services at Risk, 250+ Malicious Extensions, AI Server Hijacking + 17 More Stories

AI 导读

The Hacker News ThreatsDay 汇总本周 20 条安全事件：Todoist 称 11 月 23 日攻击者用被盗凭据入侵其生产系统，影响 6% 用户。

正文 · 原文

A website can look perfectly fine to the person running it. Log out, visit the same page, and things might look different. Something similar is happening with browser extensions, where even the way you find and install one can change what it does. Checking whether software works as expected only tells you so much.

Then there’s the question of who gets to use what. Stolen logins are opening doors across connected systems, while some AI servers are sitting online for anyone to use at someone else’s expense. Even software responsible for keeping the power on has found its way into an attack.

Meanwhile, authorities are taking down large scam operations, extortion gangs are making comebacks, and ransomware activity is picking up. Some of the more worrying findings this week involve things most people would have little reason to suspect. The details are worth a look.

The threats change every week. Subscribe, and we’ll alert you when each new ThreatsDay Bulletin is out.

  1. Stolen credentials expose customer data

    Todoist has disclosed that an attacker gained unauthorized access to its production systems and retrieved some customer information on November 23, 2026. The incident affects 6% of its users. "Between November 22 and 23, an attacker used a stolen credential associated with our version control system to modify automated workflows in three repositories and obtain additional credentials," it said. During this time, they could also read our source code. Our investigation found no changes to application code. One of the credentials they obtained allowed them to change how employees access our internal systems. The attacker used it to take over an employee’s work account and gain access to our cloud infrastructure. On November 23, the attacker downloaded a file containing account information for some early Todoist users whose passwords were stored using an older hashing method. They also made targeted queries for account tokens, searched some customer content for credentials, and accessed email information through connected Todoist for Outlook accounts."

  2. Record cybersecurity acquisition

    CrowdStrike said it will acquire Zscaler in a deal worth $43.9 billion, making it the biggest cybersecurity acquisition on record. "Security used to be about keeping the adversary out. Today, safety is required to let AI in," CrowdStrike CEO George Kurtz said. "The network used to connect people to places. Now it connects agents to data. With Zscaler, we're building cybersecurity's operating system: a single control plane to deploy AI, control AI, and enable any agentic application to run safely. We see a path to become the largest AI safety and security company the world has ever seen."

  3. Paid ads activate hidden payloads

    Microsoft's Edge Extensions Security Team has documented SleeperWidget, a set of over 250 extensions across Microsoft Edge Add-ons, Chrome Web Store, and Mozilla Add-ons that work as advertised unless installed through a paid search ad. Once installed that way, they collect browsing data, inject ads, and change shopping links. "Its extensions pose as maps, recipe finders, package trackers, and other everyday tools. Most deliver the features they advertise, which makes hidden behavior easier to miss," it said. "In the extensions we analyzed, installation through a paid-advertising funnel also activated browsing-data collection, ad injection, and shopping-link modification."

  4. LLMjacking without exploits

    An analysis of 20,354 internet-facing hosts has revealed that thousands of servers where AI services or notebook APIs have been deployed and left accessible without authentication, potentially enabling attackers to abuse them for their own purposes. "More than half of the exposed Ollama servers in this scan have already been touched by someone who is not the owner," Rapid7 said. "79% of exposed Ollama hosts list at least one :cloud model, mostly GLM, DeepSeek, MiniMax and Kimi variants. An exposed, signed-in server can provide access to someone else's paid subscription, with the bill landing on the owner. This is the self-hosted version of LLMjacking, and it requires no exploit at all."

  5. Blockchain-backed malware delivery

    SonicWall Capture Labs has disclosed details of a Java-based loader from SilentNet, a Java-based remote access trojan and infostealer sold as a malware-as-a-service offering that's advertised on YouTube. The JAR masquerades as a mod for Minecraft Fabric and targets players who download mods from external sources. "Once loaded by the game, it pulls the player's Minecraft session token from memory. It then gets its command-and-control (C2) address from a smart contract on the Polygon blockchain, and downloads a portable Python runtime hidden inside a PNG image to run a second-stage infostealer," SoniWall said. "Every string in the JAR is hidden by per-character arithmetic; it resolves hostnames through DNS-over-HTTPS, and it talks to its C2 through a custom HTTPS client that skips certificate validation."

  6. AI-powered phishing infrastructure

    Threat actors are using DeepSite, Hugging Face's AI website builder, to create dozens of phishing and credential-stealing pages on Hugging Face Spaces impersonating 28 brands, including Microsoft, Google, PayPal, Chase, and Coinbase. "29 of the pages were built with DeepSite, Hugging Face’s AI website builder," Aikido said. "One Microsoft login clone took about 90 minutes of plain-language prompts, and other operators asked the builder to hide their pages from crawlers or send stolen logins to Telegram."

  7. Credential theft spans 127 CVEs

    According to VulnCheck, requests to steal credentials made up 13% of all vulnerability exploitation recorded between January and November 2026. Credential-harvesting attempts appeared across 127 of 694 CVEs observed in its honeypot network, with open-source software and AI developer tools like Apache, Laravel, Langflow, Flowise, MLflow, and n8n taking up 61% of the CVEs associated with credential-harvesting attempts. "Attackers search application files, logs and environment variables for database passwords, cloud credentials and API keys, with file-read and SSRF vulnerabilities providing opportunities without code execution," VulnCheck said, adding it "observed 1,756 attempts targeting AI provider and coding agent credentials from 406 IP addresses across 27 CVEs."

  8. Ransomware disrupts critical services

    A previously unknown ransomware and data-extortion group calling itself BYOD has claimed at least nine organizations between October 5 and November 9, 2026. "Its attacks have had real-world consequences, including ambulance diversion, more than 30 clinics closed, and campus-wide device shutdowns," TrendAI said. "BYOD runs a pressure campaign alongside the intrusion: audio messages on infected computers, public leak-site posts, statements framing its attacks as holding organizations accountable, and outreach to individual victims." Although the complete intrusion chain remains to be analyzed, evidence points to credential compromise and a remote access trojan on an employee's computer for initial access.

  9. Malicious JavaScript delivery platform

    A malware campaign has been observed installing a fake WordPress plugin built almost entirely by a name generator, including its title, directories, files, and functions (e.g., trijavascriptly, ultravueal, maxdataic, and quadmicroservicesion). "Behind the generated names is a second-stage delivery platform that runs arbitrary JavaScript in the browser of every anonymous visitor to an infected site, routes that traffic through the victim's own domain, and lies dormant for logged-in users," Wordfence said. The malware has been discovered twice, once in January 2026 and another in September 2026. Exactly how the plugin is installed is not known, but investigation shows that it's likely via a compromise of an administrator account.

  10. Global scam networks disrupted

    The U.S. Department of Justice (DoJ) has announced that over 17.3 million scam accounts have been deplatformed on Meta, 700 fraudulent crypto investment websites seized, and 12 U.S. arrest warrants issued as part of efforts to tackle foreign and domestic scams over the past six months. The Strike Force has traced and restrained $1,082,260,156.82 in cryptocurrency stolen from U.S. victims. "Our Scam Center Strike Force has continued its work investigating cyber-enabled fraud perpetrated against Americans by transnational organized crime groups, emanating out of Asia and attacking America's main street," said U.S. Attorney Jeanine Ferris Pirro. "The Strike Force teams focus on identifying and pursuing key leaders — including Chinese organized crime affiliates operating in Cambodia, Laos, and Burma, and rapidly expanding around the globe — to bring them to justice." FBI Director Kash Patel said the Operation Blackout scam demolition initiative has "seized nearly $17 billion in illicit funds, taken more than 14,000 digital devices, directly warned over 11,000 Americans from scam attempts, and prevented more than $690 million in potential victim losses." Over 29 scam compounds operating across Asia, Africa, and the Middle East have been dismantled and more than 2,200 trafficked workers have been repatriated. The U.S. State Department has also worked with U.S. and Southeast Asian law enforcement to facilitate the freezing of more than $1 billion in stolen cryptocurrency, the takedown of over 1.4 million fraudulent websites and accounts, and several arrests. Earlier this month, the U.S. Attorney's Office also filed a civil forfeiture action to recover 456,602 USDT (Tether) that's alleged to be proceeds of romance fraud schemes targeting individuals in Massachusetts and elsewhere on online dating sites and Facebook.

  11. Billion-dollar crypto recovery bid

    The Justice Department has filed a civil action to recover over $1.1 billion in cryptocurrency that was illicitly obtained from Binance by North Korean actors in a major hack last month. "To date, the United States has assisted in the freezing, seizing, and/or the return of almost $3 billion worth of cryptocurrency from multiple sources since the theft from Binance on or about October 29," the DoJ said. Brett Leatherman, Cyber Branch Operations Director of the FBI, said, "North Korea depends on sophisticated laundering techniques to convert stolen cryptocurrency into funds that support the regime's illicit activities," adding, "Following the Binance theft, the FBI and our partners moved quickly to trace and freeze stolen assets and share critical information with virtual asset service providers."

  12. Extortion gang resurfaces after arrests

    The ShinyHunters extortion gang claims to have returned on a new data leak site following a series of arrests targeting alleged members. The new TOR site, launched on November 23, features messages in both Russian and English announcing the group's return, while denouncing a former member, Rey (aka Saif), who was reportedly arrested and is "actively cooperating" with law enforcement. "We want to let you know that from now on we will only be using our account on Exploit, which we've held since 2020," the group said. "We won't be active on any other BF-style forums anymore." They also concluded the statement with a shout-out to two other arrested members, Umbreon and Ed Dubrovsky. "Thank you for being with us all this time," the group said. "We won't forget you, and we might even send you a couple of postcards to your rat-infested cells. It was a pleasure working with you. And it's truly a shame that our guys got caught because of such silly mistakes, ones that could easily have been avoided if you fools hadn't gotten involved with the wrong people."

  13. AI misuse detection without data retention

    Anthropic has launched a public beta of Enterprise Frontier Safeguards (EFS), allowing organizations to detect potentially harmful AI activity across multiple interactions while maintaining zero data retention (ZDR). The protection employs a second defensive layer, known as automated safety monitoring, that looks for patterns of misuse across multiple requests to provide automated safety monitoring without storing any customer data. "Instead, our automated safety monitoring system analyzes data stored entirely on infrastructure controlled by our customers," Anthropic said. "When automated safety monitoring finds a risk, it creates a Safety Case for you to review. These cases can be read from your cloud storage or through an API, and can be integrated with security information and event management systems. They do not include inputs and outputs. Once it's set up, automated safety monitoring can help your team detect intentional misuse that could lead to serious harm (like offensive cyber operations). It can also help detect agents that take actions beyond a user's intentions."

  14. Ransomware activity jumps 23%

    NCC Group has revealed that ransomware attacks surged 23% in October 2026 compared to September, with 1049 claimed incidents. The Gentlemen, N0n, Qilin, Akira, and Silent Ransom have claimed the most victims. Top targeted verticals included industrials, consumer discretionary, healthcare, IT, and consumer supplies. Check Point Research has reported a record 2,830 ransomware victims in Q3 2026, claimed by 117 ransomware brands, up from 91 in the previous quarter. Of these, 42 are new. "Ransom payments continued to diverge: average payments fell 45% quarter over quarter to just over $1 million, while the median rose 167% to $400,000," Check Point said. "The top 10 groups accounted for just 49% of victims, the first time groups outside the top ten collectively represented the majority." Qilin, The Gentlemen, and INC Ransom emerged as the most active groups during the period. Interestingly, while the top 10 groups accounted for 71% of victims in Q1 2026, the figure has declined to 58% in Q2 and only 49% in Q3, indicating a fragmentation of the threat landscape. 

  15. 12,200 online stores compromised

    A new analysis from Guardio Labs has revealed that one online shop in 195 (i.e., about 12,200 of the 2.38 million stores) was hijacked in October 2026. The phenomenon has been codenamed CartJacked. "Three schemes go straight after shoppers: hand-offs to gambling and scam pages, fake 'I'm not a robot' checks that tell you to paste a command (about 900 shops), and card skimmers," Guardio said. "Of the ten commands the fake checks handed out, three still delivered malware, including a stealer for passwords and crypto wallets."

  16. €26 million crypto trail uncovered

    A Europol-led operation along with authorities from 11 countries has disrupted 57 illegal IPTV services and the identification of 321 websites illegally offering copyrighted content as part of a five-day effort between November 16 and 20, 2026. The sites drew over 7.3 million visits each year. More than €26 million in cryptocurrency transactions have been traced so far. "The operation targeted a criminal market worth billions of euros annually, in which illegal streaming providers sell unauthorized access to premium television channels, live sports and films, often collecting subscription payments through cryptocurrency," Europol said. "The profits generated by these services do not simply disappear. They move through payment accounts, cryptocurrency wallets and intermediaries, making it harder for investigators to identify who is ultimately benefiting." No arrests have been announced.

  17. 10 million exposed NGINX services at risk

    Censys has revealed that more than 10 million exposed NGINX services are running an affected version with HTTP/2 that are vulnerable to a set of flaws called NGINX2Root that "could allow a remote, unauthenticated attacker to corrupt memory in an NGINX worker process when a set of specific features are enabled – leading to code execution where ASLR is disabled or the attacker can bypass it."

  18. Power management software weaponized

    Broadcom-owned Symantec has disclosed details of an intrusion in which the attacker used CyberPower PowerPanel Business (PPB), a software that manages UPS devices, as a way to execute code on a domain controller, dump Active Directory secrets and launch a persistent implant. "PPB can run a script (a .cmd file in an extcmd folder) when a power-related event such as a voltage sag or outage is detected," Symantec said. "The attacker placed their own scripts in that folder through the product's web console and had the PowerPanel service, ppbed.exe, run them. The commands in this attack chain have ppbed.exe as their ancestor and run as SYSTEM. The attacker used this channel to download tools with certutil, then loaded a .NET assembly into memory using PowerShell, and ran ntdsutil and esentutl against the Active Directory database, ntds.dit." The same channel has also been used to launch an implant ("upnpcont.exe") that uses DLL sideloading to launch a suspicious DLL ("atl.dll"). "What this attack shows is that UPS, power-management, and other infrastructure software should be treated like any other potentially dual-use RMM product; used with network restrictions, unique credentials, vigilant patching, and process-level monitoring," Symantec said.

  19. Banking middleware weaponized

    A new campaign called BankWeaver has targeted a Temenos Quantum Fabric deployment with an exposed authentication service that was configured to accept anonymous access to obtain administrator privileges and deliver malicious Java components via the platform's extension mechanism. The attack targeted an unspecified financial services institution in early September 2026. "Attackers could generate an anonymous token by accessing a public application and then exchange it for an administrator token on another exposed application," Forescout said. "The new token obtained by the attackers granted access to administrative functions within the Temenos Fabric platform, including managing running applications and creating new services." The malicious components delivered as part of the attack include: FabricShell, which provides arbitrary command execution and database queries, and FabricShadow, which collects host information, stages output locally, and exfiltrates results to remote servers. Temenos has since released updates and guidance to configure the service as private and advised customers to restrict internet access to Spotlight and Fabric administrative and authentication services.

  20. Fake updates deliver Python backdoor

    A new wave of Microsoft Teams social engineering activity exhibiting similarities to Edgecution was uncovered by Arctic Wolf Labs last month. "In the October wave, following a lull in observed cases from August to September, new delivery artifacts and operational patterns were observed," the cybersecurity company said. "The campaign abuses a malicious Edge extension and Chrome Native Messaging to run a Python backdoor outside the browser sandbox. The activity shares early chain tradecraft with PREY-0059, including external-tenant Teams impersonation of IT support, legitimate runtimes paired with scripts, and S3-hosted payloads disguised as legitimate software. While PREY-0059 used S3 buckets named after targeted victims and delivered a Java-based RAT alongside a Java runtime, the current activity used generic Microsoft-themed tenants and buckets, an Edge extension, and a Python backdoor paired with a Python runtime."

One thing worth taking away from this week's stories is how much damage can happen without a particularly clever attack. A forgotten server, a stolen credential, or a feature nobody thought to monitor can be enough. AI may be changing how some attacks are carried out, but many of the ways attackers get in are still surprisingly ordinary.

The harder part is knowing what deserves attention before it becomes a problem. That includes software sitting quietly in the background, services exposed for convenience, and accounts with more access than they need. Somewhere between keeping things running and adding new capabilities, it's easy to lose track of what you've already put online. This week offers several good reasons to look again.
